# Straight Up: trademark paths and risk assessment

**Date:** 2026-07-16  
**Status:** Candidate-specific research; not a clearance opinion or legal advice  
**Proposed use:** Premium non-medicated facial skincare, beginning with a barrier-repair cream and potentially expanding to serum and adjacent skincare products

## Bottom line

`STRAIGHT UP` is **not automatically unavailable for U.S. facial skincare**, but it is not a clean or especially ownable name.

The automated knockout screen was too blunt because it treated an exact Class 3 registration as if it covered cosmetics. It does not: Buckeye International's active `STRAIGHT-UP` registration covers only **pH-neutral all-purpose cleaning preparations**. The USPTO has also previously approved the exact words `STRAIGHT UP` for a long list of skin- and hair-care products; that application died only because the applicant never filed proof of use.

The harder problem is the later, active `STRAIGHT UP` registration for **hair styling** associated with the Drybar/KAZ/Helen of Troy ownership chain. Hair styling in Class 44 is beauty-adjacent to Class 3 skincare, and Class 44 is a USPTO-coordinated search class for Class 3. Drybar also still actively presents **The Straight Up** as a signature hairstyle. A new applicant would therefore have a credible argument for coexistence, but no dependable right to registration without either persuading the examiner or obtaining a strong consent agreement.

There is a second, business-level issue: the phrase is crowded and already used as skincare messaging. Ursa Major currently says **“Skincare, straight up.”** E45 has used **“straight-up skincare”** as a brand platform, and a South African trademark journal published `STRAIGHT UP SKINCARE` applications owned by Reckitt & Colman (Overseas) Limited. Crowding can narrow an existing owner's scope, but it also makes our own mark harder to monopolize, search for, and enforce.

An expansion review of supplements, ingestible beauty, anti-aging devices, and treatment services materially worsens the umbrella-brand case. There is no current exact U.S. registration in Class 5 supplements or Class 10 medical devices, but there are active exact marks for beauty hardware and beverages, historical exact supplement registrations, active foreign supplement brands, and an adverse FDA search association. `STRAIGHT UP` may remain arguable for narrow facial skincare; it is **not recommended as a multi-category longevity master brand** on the current evidence.

### Manual verdict

| Question | Assessment |

## What the live records actually cover

### 1. Buckeye International — relevant by class number, remote by goods

- **Mark:** `STRAIGHT-UP`
- **Serial / registration:** 75576297 / 2292768
- **Status:** Active and renewed
- **Filed / registered:** 1998 / 1999
- **Identification:** “all purpose cleaning preparations which are pH neutral”
- **Current marketplace use:** Buckeye sells Straight-Up as a pH-neutral floor and general cleaner in institutional formats including gallon and drum packaging.

This is the clearest reason the preliminary `RED` label was overstated. The USPTO says international classification has no bearing on likelihood of confusion; the wording of the identified goods and services controls. Cosmetics and cleaning preparations both happen to sit in Class 3, but industrial floor cleaner and facial moisturizer are not automatically related merely because the class number matches.

**Residual risk:** Low to moderate as a registration obstacle, and low as a marketplace-confusion risk on the current facts. An examiner could still investigate relatedness, and Buckeye could object, but the existing record gives a strong basis to distinguish the goods, buyers, channels, packaging, and commercial impression.

### 2. Drybar/KAZ/Helen of Troy chain — the principal U.S. issue

- **Mark:** `STRAIGHT UP`
- **Serial / registration:** 85672186 / 4279932
- **Status:** Active and renewed
- **Filed / registered:** 2012 / 2013
- **Identification:** “Hair styling”
- **Claimed first use:** 2010
- **Current marketplace use:** Drybar currently promotes **The Straight Up** as a signature straight-with-body hairstyle and uses the page to merchandise hair products and tools.

The marks would be identical in sound, appearance, meaning, and commercial impression. The only meaningful registration argument is that non-medicated facial skincare goods and a named hair-styling service are sufficiently different in nature, field of use, purchaser expectation, and actual presentation.

That argument is credible but uncertain. Beauty companies and salons can sell related products; the same consumers buy both; hair and skincare can share retailers and marketing; and USPTO search guidance coordinates Class 3 with Class 44. The registrant also has the benefit of any examiner doubt.

Public owner views are not perfectly aligned: third-party records show Helen of Troy Limited in the ownership history, while other current record views display KAZ Europe Sarl. Counsel should pull and reconcile the USPTO assignment chain before any approach. For strategy purposes, this memo refers to the **Drybar/KAZ/Helen of Troy chain**.

**Residual risk:** Moderate to high as a registration obstacle; moderate as a marketplace/opposition risk. This is the party whose consent would most improve the path.

### 3. The unusually helpful 2011 skincare application

An earlier applicant, Real Advertising and Marketing LLC, filed exact standard-character `STRAIGHT UP`, serial 85430886, for products including:

- cosmetic preparations for skin care;
- moisturizing preparations for the skin;
- non-medicated skin-care creams and lotions;
- skin cream, lotion, moisturizer, and conditioner; and
- extensive hair-care and styling preparations.

The January 2012 Office Action expressly said **“No Similar Marks Found”** and that the examiner found no conflicting marks under Section 2(d). The only objection concerned indefinite wording in one product-kit identification. The applicant amended the wording, the application was approved and published, and a Notice of Allowance issued. It was abandoned in 2015 only because no Statement of Use was filed.

This is valuable factual evidence that Buckeye's cleaner registration did not prevent the USPTO from approving identical wording for skincare. It is **not precedent**: USPTO examining decisions are not binding, every application turns on its own record, and the application had an earlier filing date than the Drybar application. It therefore does not answer the current Drybar problem.

## Governing rules that matter here

1. **A matching class number is not the legal test.** Likelihood of confusion depends primarily on similarity of the marks and relatedness of the identified goods/services. Different classes can conflict; the same class can coexist.
2. **The words are effectively identical.** `StraightUp`, `Straight Up`, and `Straight-Up` have the same pronunciation and commercial impression. Spacing or punctuation does not create a meaningful escape route.
3. **A stylized logo does not create freedom to use the words.** A standard-character registration covers the wording without limitation to font, size, or color. In word-plus-design marks, wording is often treated as the dominant way consumers request the goods.
4. **Adding descriptive matter is usually weak.** `STRAIGHT UP SKINCARE`, `STRAIGHT UP SKIN`, or similar additions may leave `STRAIGHT UP` dominant. An additional inherently distinctive element can help only if the whole mark creates a genuinely different commercial impression and the new composite is itself cleared.
5. **A consent agreement can be powerful, but a naked consent is not enough.** The USPTO gives more weight to an agreement that explains why confusion is unlikely and imposes concrete field-of-use, channel, presentation, and confusion-avoidance obligations.
6. **A prior examiner's approval helps factually but is not binding.** It should support the distinction from Buckeye, not be treated as a guarantee.
7. **Federal approval is not complete marketplace clearance.** An unregistered user can retain common-law rights, and an owner can oppose after publication. Domain, company-name, and social-handle availability do not answer trademark availability.

## Lawful paths, ranked

### Path A — Narrow U.S. intent-to-use filing plus a Drybar consent strategy

**Best path if the exact name is important.**

Have U.S. trademark counsel complete a comprehensive search and, if the results remain consistent with this memo, file a Section 1(b) intent-to-use application for the standard-character mark. Keep the identification truthful and narrow, for example around **non-medicated facial skincare preparations; facial moisturizer; facial skin cream** rather than claiming every cosmetic, personal-care, salon, and hair product the company might someday sell.

The filing can test USPTO examination before the company commits to launch packaging, and its filing date can establish priority against later filers. It does not establish priority over Drybar's existing rights.

If Drybar is cited—or if counsel believes an opposition is sufficiently likely—seek a detailed, signed consent/coexistence agreement. A strong agreement should address:

- our use only for identified non-medicated facial skincare goods;
- no `STRAIGHT UP` hair-care products, hair tools, hair-styling services, salons, or blow-dry bars;
- no expansion into the registrant's signature-style naming convention;
- distinct packaging, trade dress, typography, and brand presentation;
- a factually honest description of any separate trade channels, while recognizing that beauty-retail channels may overlap;
- no suggestion of affiliation, sponsorship, or collaboration;
- procedures to report, investigate, and correct actual confusion;
- mutual consent to the specified applications/registrations and appropriate no-challenge language;
- rules for future product expansion and a notice/approval mechanism;
- binding effect on affiliates, successors, and assignees.

**Important sequencing question:** Contacting a registrant can invite monitoring, a payment demand, or opposition. Counsel should decide whether to approach before filing, immediately after filing, or only after an Office Action. The right sequence depends on the assignment chain, the registrant's enforcement history, and how much launch spend is already committed.

**Assessment:** Credible, not bankable. A well-designed consent materially improves the odds; an examiner may still weigh all circumstances.

### Path B — Distinctive composite or house mark with `STRAIGHT UP` secondary

Use a separately cleared, inherently distinctive house mark as the dominant source indicator and make `Straight Up` secondary. This is materially more promising than adding the descriptive word `SKINCARE`, but it works only if the new whole creates a different commercial impression and is used consistently as a unit.

Examples should be generated only after setting a naming structure; no unsearched example in a brainstorming document should be treated as available.

**Assessment:** Lower risk than the exact standalone name, but it gives up some of the desired simplicity and still requires a fresh search. Merely writing `HOUSE MARK / STRAIGHT UP` in small type may not be enough if customers still call the product line “Straight Up.”

### Path C — Different primary brand; “straight up” used only as ordinary copy

Choose a different protectable brand and use the phrase descriptively in occasional messaging, not as a product or line name. This can reduce source-confusion risk because ordinary language is less likely to function as the brand.

However, the exact skincare messaging lane is already crowded. Ursa Major currently uses **“Skincare, straight up.”**, and E45 has used **“straight-up skincare.”** We should avoid those formulations and should not expect exclusive rights in the concept.

**Assessment:** Lowest-risk way to preserve the tone, but it does not preserve `Straight Up` as the brand.

### Path D — Acquire or license rights

In theory, the company could seek a license, assignment, or broader commercial deal with the Drybar-side registrant. A trademark assignment must include the associated goodwill; purchasing a registration as a detached piece of paper is not a valid shortcut. A license also needs appropriate quality-control terms.

Buying Buckeye's industrial-cleaner mark would not automatically create skincare rights and would not solve the Drybar registration. Buying the Drybar-side mark and associated goodwill would likely be commercially disproportionate and could pull the company toward a hair-service business it does not want.

**Assessment:** Legally possible, commercially unlikely. A targeted consent is the more realistic transaction.

### Path E — Challenge or cancel the existing registrations

Both principal registrations are more than five years old, renewed, and supported by visible current use. The broad five-year cancellation window is closed. Limited grounds such as abandonment, genericness, fraud, or never-use can remain available in particular circumstances, but this research found no factual basis for them. The statute also permits a never-used claim after three years, but visible current use defeats the obvious theory here.

**Assessment:** Poor path on current evidence. Do not budget around cancellation leverage.

### Path F — Geographic concurrent use

Concurrent-use registrations can divide rights geographically when parties have good-faith regional priority. A new DTC skincare company planning national e-commerce has no useful geographic separation from an established national beauty brand and no earlier regional use to preserve.

**Assessment:** Not a practical fit.

## Approaches that do not solve the problem

- `StraightUp` instead of `Straight Up`
- `Straight-Up` instead of `Straight Up`
- filing only a logo while using the words prominently
- adding only `SKIN`, `SKINCARE`, `BEAUTY`, or `LABS`
- applying on the Supplemental Register to evade Section 2(d)
- incorporating an LLC, buying a domain, or obtaining social handles
- launching without filing and assuming lack of registration means lack of infringement
- buying the Buckeye registration without the relevant business/goodwill
- relying on the abandoned 2011 application as a legal precedent

## Expansion analysis: supplements and anti-aging devices

### Search scope and class map

On 2026-07-16, the official USPTO search was rerun for exact and compact/phonetic `STRAIGHT UP`, `STRAIGHTUP`, `STRAIGHT-UP`, and `STR8UP` forms, including live and dead records. The review covered the likely expansion classes rather than assuming one “health” class:

- **Class 5:** dietary and nutritional supplements, including many ingestible collagen products;
- **Class 10:** medical, therapeutic, and some aesthetic-treatment devices;
- **Classes 8, 11, and 21:** particular grooming tools, lighting apparatus, and non-medical cosmetic utensils, depending on the product's function;
- **Class 32:** non-alcoholic beverages and preparations for making them;
- **Classes 9 and 42:** downloadable apps, software, and technology services associated with connected devices;
- **Class 35:** retail services; and
- **Class 44:** medical, beauty, spa, and treatment services.

Classification remains administrative rather than dispositive. An exact mark on related goods can conflict across classes, and products in one class can be sufficiently different to coexist.

### Expansion risk matrix

| Proposed expansion | Exact/current evidence | Practical rating |
|---|---|---|
| Supplement capsules or tablets | No live exact U.S. Class 5 record found. Two exact Class 5 registrations are cancelled. Active foreign supplement uses remain. | **AMBER-RED** — filing is possible, but the name has poor history and international ownability. |
| Collagen powder, beauty drink, or ingestible drink mix | Active exact `STRAIGHT UP` Class 32 registration and operating Straight Up Beverages business; active Australian Straight Up collagen/supplement sellers. | **RED** for an umbrella launch without additional consent and territory work. |
| LED mask, microcurrent device, or powered facial device | No live exact Class 10 or 11 record found; active exact `STRAIGHT UP` hair-styling device registration and product create close beauty-hardware adjacency. | **AMBER-RED** — a separate owner and likely separate coexistence problem. |
| Gua sha, facial roller, or non-electric cosmetic tool | No live exact Class 21 record found in this search; still adjacent to the active beauty-hardware and skincare cluster. | **AMBER** — narrower argument, but not clean. |
| Connected-device app | A 2026 exact `STRAIGHT UP` Class 9 application covers payment software, not health software. | **AMBER** — likely distinguishable by goods, but another exact record to address. |
| Medspa, facial treatment, longevity clinic, or aesthetic service | Active exact Drybar registration for hair styling in Class 44. | **RED** absent a carefully scoped consent; services materially increase the Drybar problem. |

### Supplements: no live exact Class 5 bar, but a bad brand environment

The current federal search found no live exact `STRAIGHT UP` registration or application for dietary supplements. It did find two dead exact records:

1. serial **78779722**, registration **3168584**, for “dietary supplements,” registered in 2006 and cancelled in 2013; and
2. serial **86396996**, registration **4868011**, for a pre-workout energy and endurance nutritional supplement, registered in 2015 and later cancelled.

Dead registrations cannot block a new application by themselves. The first record is nevertheless a major reputational problem. The former owner, Novacare LLC, recalled a U.S. supplement sold as **Straight Up** in 2010, and the FDA's current Health Fraud Product Database lists it for undeclared sulfoaildenafil. This does not create rights for the old owner, but it is an unusually poor search association for a science- and trust-led longevity brand.

The name is also in active supplement use abroad:

- **Straight Up Supplements** sells supplements and collagen in Australia; and
- **Straight Up Performance** sells collagen peptides and other evidence-positioned sports supplements in Australia, including skin, hair, and nail benefit messaging.

Those businesses have not been established here as owners of U.S. rights, and their Australian trademark status was not verified. They still create direct international, SEO, marketplace-confusion, and expansion problems. Australia should be treated as a high-risk territory unless local counsel proves otherwise.

**Conclusion for supplements:** A narrow U.S. Class 5 filing for capsules may be arguable, but the name is strategically poor for supplements. The adverse FDA legacy and active Australian collagen brands are more damaging to brand trust than the live-register result suggests.

### Ingestible beauty and beverages: a current exact U.S. owner

Irish Carbonic owns active registration **5692972**, serial **87703324**, for exact standard-character `STRAIGHT UP` covering bulk beverage syrups. It actively operates Straight Up Beverages, offering soda, teas, lemonades, mixers, and other fountain products. Public company messaging has also referenced energy drinks.

Capsule supplements and commercial soda syrup are distinguishable. The problem becomes substantially harder if the proposed product is:

- a collagen or electrolyte powder mixed into beverages;
- a functional beauty drink;
- a ready-to-drink longevity beverage;
- an energy or hydration drink; or
- a liquid supplement sold through ordinary beverage channels.

In those formats, the mark is identical, both products are consumable beverages or beverage preparations, and channels and purchasers can overlap. Class 5 instead of Class 32 would not cure that relatedness.

**Conclusion for drink formats:** Treat as a likely blocker or consent requirement. A Drybar consent would not solve the Irish Carbonic rights.

### Devices: active exact beauty hardware changes the analysis

Trade Box, LLC owns active registration **5143238**, serial **86919613**, for exact standard-character `STRAIGHT UP` covering electric irons for styling hair. The product remains on sale as the InStyler **STRAIGHT UP Straightening Brush**.

This record is more important to a facial device than its Class 8 number suggests. A red-light mask, microcurrent wand, sonic cleansing device, or other home beauty device could be sold to the same consumers through the same beauty retailers and could reasonably be perceived as another device from the same source. The factual distinction—hair styling versus facial skincare treatment—is real, but the channels and product form are much closer than Buckeye's floor cleaner.

No live exact Class 10 or Class 11 record surfaced. The exact Class 10 application at serial **98072002** was for teeth aligners and is abandoned, so it is not a present federal bar. That favorable result does not neutralize the active Trade Box beauty-device registration.

**Conclusion for devices:** A facial-device filing is arguable but should be treated as a second consent/clearance project. Any coexistence agreement would need to reserve facial skin devices while excluding hair-styling irons, brushes, dryers, and hair-treatment devices. The entity to approach would be Trade Box/InStyler, not merely Drybar.

### Services: the weakest expansion lane

An anti-aging clinic, medspa, facial-treatment studio, or branded aesthetic service would move the business directly into beauty services. That makes the existing exact Drybar hair-styling registration more dangerous, not less. The live `STRAIGHTUP COUNSELING` registration for mental-health therapy is comparatively remote because the full mark and service field are different, but it adds to the crowded health-service landscape.

**Conclusion for services:** Do not plan a `STRAIGHT UP` clinic or treatment-service expansion without an express Drybar-side consent covering the specific service field.

### Multi-party consent reality

The original skincare-only strategy centered on one meaningful consent target. A broad longevity platform could require separate solutions with:

- **Drybar/KAZ/Helen of Troy** for beauty and styling services;
- **Trade Box/InStyler** for powered beauty hardware;
- **Irish Carbonic/Straight Up Beverages** for ingestible drinks or drink preparations; and
- potentially Buckeye for belt-and-suspenders treatment of the Class 3 cleaner record.

One owner's consent does not bind another. This fragmentation is the clearest reason `STRAIGHT UP` is a poor umbrella mark even if a narrow skincare registration can be obtained.

### Filing implication

Do not add Class 5, 10, 21, 32, or 44 merely to “reserve” them. A Section 1(b) application requires a bona fide, documented intent to use the mark for every listed good or service. If the supplement or device roadmap is real and sufficiently developed, counsel can decide whether simultaneous narrow applications are justified. If the roadmap is exploratory, a more distinctive master brand is the safer way to preserve future optionality.

## Marketplace and ownability risks beyond registration

### Weakness cuts both ways

Many unrelated owners have registered or applied for `STRAIGHT UP` in other fields. The phrase also has an ordinary-language meaning: candid, direct, or honest. Evidence of substantial similar use can support a narrower scope for an older registration. But even a weak registration is protected against confusingly similar junior use, and crowding also means our own enforcement position would be narrow.

For the brand, that likely means:

- harder SEO and paid-search efficiency;
- more domain and social-handle compromises;
- more marketplace monitoring noise;
- less ability to stop others from using “straight up” descriptively;
- greater dependence on packaging, a house mark, and trade dress for differentiation; and
- a weaker long-term naming moat than a distinctive coined or unexpected mark.

Exact `.com` and handle availability were not verified in this research and should not be assumed.

### International warning

The October 2018 South African trademark journal published `STRAIGHT UP SKINCARE` filings for Reckitt & Colman (Overseas) Limited covering non-medicated and medicated skincare. The journal also included a disclaimer of exclusive rights in `STRAIGHT` and `SKINCARE` apart from the mark. This is not a current-status certificate, so South African counsel should verify whether the applications registered and remain in force. Until then, treat South Africa as a likely high-risk territory.

No mark is “globally cleared.” Canada, the UK, EU, Australia, and any other launch or manufacturing markets require current, country-specific searches. The Madrid Protocol is a filing mechanism, not a universal clearance or registration.

## Recommended decision gates

### Gate 1 — Is the name worth a narrow moat?

If the team wants a highly ownable global brand, easy digital discoverability, and broad expansion into hair, supplements, devices, retail services, or clinics, stop here and choose another name.

If the emotional and strategic fit of `Straight Up` is unusually high and the company can accept a narrower facial-skincare field, proceed.

### Gate 2 — Attorney full search

Commission a comprehensive U.S. search that includes:

- live and dead federal records;
- state registrations and business names;
- unregistered skincare, beauty, salon, wellness, and personal-care uses;
- phonetic and meaning equivalents;
- the Drybar assignment chain and enforcement/opposition history;
- common retail and channel evidence connecting skincare goods to salon/hair services; and
- Ursa Major, E45/Reckitt, and any other direct-category “straight up” usage.

Ask counsel for separate conclusions on **registrability**, **freedom to use**, **opposition risk**, and **enforcement strength**. Those are not the same question.

### Gate 3 — File narrowly before irreversible spend

If counsel supports the path, file the U.S. Section 1(b) standard-character application with a deliberately limited, accurate identification. Preserve dated evidence of bona fide intent: formulation work, manufacturer discussions, packaging development, budgets, and launch plans.

Do not order non-changeable packaging, announce the brand publicly, or build inventory around the name merely because an application has been filed. A useful commercial gate is approval and publication without opposition, or a signed coexistence agreement if the Drybar issue remains live.

### Gate 4 — Consent or exit

Before major launch spend, require one of these outcomes:

1. counsel concludes the exact name is reasonably clear after examination and opposition analysis; or
2. for a skincare-only launch, a robust Drybar-side consent/coexistence agreement is signed; for any broader launch, every material owner in the applicable field has also consented; or
3. the company switches to a separately cleared composite or new name.

If the examiner cites Drybar and the registrant will not consent, an appeal remains possible, but the expected cost, delay, and uncertain result are probably disproportionate for an early-stage brand with many available naming alternatives.

## Recommended position for the brand team

Treat `Straight Up` as a **legally plausible exception project**, not as a cleared finalist.

The exact standalone mark is worth pursuing only if the team is prepared to:

- stay tightly focused on facial skincare;
- give up `STRAIGHT UP` hair products and salon services;
- keep supplements, ingestible drinks, devices, and clinics outside the mark unless each receives a new clearance and any required consent;
- accept a relatively weak, crowded word mark;
- fund counsel-led prosecution and potential consent negotiation;
- delay irreversible packaging spend until a legal gate is passed; and
- perform territory-by-territory international clearance.

If those constraints feel inconsistent with the intended brand platform or future product roadmap, the rational answer is not to “work around” the registrations. It is to choose a more distinctive name now. If the intended destination is a multi-category longevity company rather than a skincare-only company, this memo recommends changing the master brand.

## Counsel handoff: the eight questions to answer

1. Would the current `STRAIGHT UP` hair-styling registration likely be cited against the narrowest truthful facial-skincare identification?
2. What third-party beauty/skincare usage is strong enough to narrow the Drybar registration without simultaneously making our own mark fail to function or become too weak to enforce?
3. Should we contact the current registrant before filing, after filing, or only after a refusal—and which entity in the assignment chain has authority to consent?
4. What identification and coexistence restrictions reduce relatedness without crippling the actual three-year product roadmap?
5. Do Ursa Major, Reckitt/E45, or any unregistered U.S. user have enforceable priority that creates use or opposition risk even if the USPTO does not cite them?
6. Would Trade Box/InStyler likely block a facial LED, microcurrent, sonic, or other powered beauty device under exact `STRAIGHT UP`?
7. Would Irish Carbonic's exact beverage registration block collagen powders, drink mixes, ready-to-drink supplements, or other ingestible beauty formats?
8. Does the Novacare/FDA history create enough reputational or search risk that Class 5 should be abandoned regardless of technical registrability?
