The repeated pattern matters more than the individual rejections: the process kept elevating boring, logical-sounding phrases whose connection to skincare existed mainly in a written explanation.

## What actually failed

### 1. Explainability was mistaken for relevance

The selection process rewarded any phrase that could be connected to confidence, transparency, strength, sensitivity, or skin health. That is a strategy presentation test, not a name test. If the skincare relationship appears only after a rationale, the naked name has already failed.

### 2. Coherence was mistaken for desire

The scoring favored calm plausibility, tidy wordplay, and strategic coverage. It underweighted the decisive reaction: **would the founder want to put this name on a premium serum bottle?** A phrase can be coherent and still have no voltage, beauty, attitude, or brand world.

### 3. The positive signals were abstracted incorrectly

`Straight Face` and `Bare Facts` did not work merely because they had double meanings. They had idiomatic snap, cultural familiarity, attitude, and an immediate relationship to faces, bareness, beauty, or candor. Cycle 2 copied the grammatical shell—two familiar words with a possible secondary reading—while losing those qualities.

That produced strategy-language and therapy-language such as `Active Voice`, `Firm Belief`, and `Strong Boundaries`, not skincare masterbrands.

### 4. Brand values were treated as naming territories

Science, transparency, efficacy, and respect are essential parts of the positioning, but the name does not need to summarize them. Trying to encode confidence, improvement, clarity, and skin relevance in a short phrase repeatedly produced slogans and abstractions.

### 5. Preliminary screening introduced a genericness bias

Collision screening was useful as a knockout check, but legally quieter phrases gained implicit credibility. Strong, culturally alive language is often crowded; bland combinations are often quieter. Availability is not evidence of creative quality and should never determine which ideas feel desirable.

## What the founder's taste evidence now says

A viable name should:

- create immediate pull before its rationale;
- feel like a desirable object and a brand world, not a statement of values;
- have verbal attitude, rhythm, surprise, or beauty;
- feel natural near premium skincare without needing `skin`, a product benefit, or a strategic explanation;
- reward a second look without making the first look confusing;
- work naked, without a logo, packaging mockup, score, or availability badge.

Fatal failures remain:

- generic two-word statements;
- relevance created only by explanation;
- obscure vocabulary and arbitrary coinages;
- faux-luxury sound without a meaningful source;
- industrial, trade, or instrument associations;
- forced `skin-` constructions;
- names that are respectable on paper but unwanted on a bottle.

## Decision

The problem is not that the Placek exercises are incapable of producing a good name. They are divergence tools: they create raw material. The failure occurred in interpretation, curation, and ranking. The process repeatedly selected for what could be defended analytically rather than what the founder could desire instinctively.

The 13,000-name corpus is now research evidence, not an active shortlist. No more candidate volume should be generated from the same brief-and-score loop. The next round should begin only after founder taste is calibrated from successful real-world brand names and should be led independently, with names judged naked before rationale or legal quietness can influence the response.
